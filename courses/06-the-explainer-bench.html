Part 0

Where we start

Both pages on the bench are constructed. The default is Wrenmoor Water's explainer, "from reservoir to your tap", in the state Nia's agency first delivered it: the parts of the cutaway named in a lettered key squeezed in under the step cards, the six steps numbered in the order they were pasted in, two cards in a pale cyan with white text, and a caption in a grey almost the colour of its panel. The second brief, Ashbrook Library's "how a reserved book reaches you", is invented too, and has its own faults to find. Nothing measured here is a fact about a real document; everything measured is computed, not assumed.

Part 1 · covers what the bench measures

How a page becomes numbers

6 min live

The bench knows the page as boxes (the title, the drawing panel, six step cards, a key if there is one, label boxes, and a decoration if you add one), parts in the drawing with a point each, and a text colour and fill for every piece of text. From those it takes six measurements, defined the same way in the browser and in an independent Python run that agrees with it on all 168 canon values.

LivePath, crossings and the entry point3 min▶

Reading path is the distance a reader's eye travels if it follows the numbers: from the entry point to the title if the title is not the entry, then step 1, step 2, and on to step 6, centre to centre. The bench also finds the shortest route through the same stops from the same entry by trying every order, so you can see how much the numbering costs. A crossing is a pair of steps on the route whose lines properly intersect; session 2 explained why a crossed route makes the reader search.

The entry point is a stated model, not a measurement: the eye enters at the largest stop whose centre sits in the top quarter of the page. It is a crude stand-in for what session 2 and the dashboard course's visual-weight rule describe, and it is printed on the widget so you can disagree with it.

LiveLabels, contrast and overlaps3 min▶

Label distance is the distance from each part's point in the drawing to the nearest edge of the words that name it: its label box when labels are direct, its row in the key when they are not. Lookups counts the names that live in a key, each one a trip away from the drawing and back (session 5's split attention). Contrast applies the WCAG 2.2 formula to every text colour on its fill and passes it at 4.5:1; all text on the bench is treated as normal size. Overlaps counts pairs of boxes that intersect without one sitting wholly inside the other, so a label inside the drawing panel is fine and a key on top of a card is not.

Build-along · the bench

Wrenmoor's one-pager, rung by rung

★ 26 min · the main event

Start on rung 1 and read the verdict line, then the six measurements, then the scorecard table at the bottom. Work down the rungs in order and say out loud what moved and what did not before you click the next one. Then drag a card somewhere silly and watch the path and the crossings change. Switch to Ashbrook Library when you want a second case.

Read rung 1 in this order. One crossing (step 1 to 2 crosses step 3 to 4), eight lookups at a mean label distance of 443.8 px, five of 37 text pairs failing (the caption at 1.27:1 and the two cyan cards at 2.01:1), three overlaps where the squeezed key sits on steps 2, 4 and 5. Score 43.1 of 100. Every one of those faults is a session you have already done.

Part 2 · covers the ladder

Four fixes, one trap, one break

6 min live

These are the numbers the bench prints on Wrenmoor's page, written down so they can be argued with after class. An independent Python run of the same steps on the same brief gives the same values to nine decimal places.

LiveRungs 2 to 4: the three fixes3 min▶

Direct labels take the mean label distance from 443.8 px to 21.3 px, lookups from eight to zero, and remove the key, which also removes all three overlaps: score 43.1 to 83.3. Renumbering moves the cards so 1 to 3 runs along the top row and 4 to 6 comes back along the bottom; the crossing goes and the path shortens from 1514 px to 1323 px: 91.5. Fixing contrast swaps white-on-cyan for ink-on-cyan (8.03:1) and the near-invisible caption for muted ink: 28 of 28 pairs pass, 95.9.

Notice what direct labels did to the contrast count: it went from 32 of 37 to 23 of 28, because the key's letters and rows left the page. The failing pairs did not change; there were just fewer passing ones around them. A share is always a share of something.

Self-studyThe second brief: Ashbrook Library3 min▶

Ashbrook's draft is worse in a different place: its six steps were numbered so the route crosses itself five times, and the path scores zero before anything else is fixed: 29.3 overall. Its contrast faults are near misses you would pass by eye, the vermilion step titles on pale pink at 4.45:1 and the grey step text on the same pink at 4.31:1. Renumbering is the big fix there (69.9 to 92.6), and the contrast fix takes it to 96.1. Same rules, different page, different order of importance.

Real world

The near miss nobody sees. 4.45:1 looks fine on a good monitor in a bright office. It is under the 4.5:1 line WCAG 2.2 sets for normal text, and it is the kind of pair that fails for a reader with low vision on a phone in daylight. Measure the pair; do not judge it by eye.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break button and the limits

Tidy is not the same as clear

6 min live

Rung 5 is the move every design review suggests at some point: "the labels look messy, put them in a neat legend". The page does look calmer. The bench counts the cost: every part is a lookup again and the mean distance from part to name goes from 21.3 px to 475.8 px. Score 95.9 to 72.7.

LiveThe anti-lever, with its honest nuance3 min▶

Spatial contiguity is the reason: Mayer and Moreno report that people understand better when printed words sit near the parts of the graphic they describe (effect size .48 in a single study, with similar effects in books). The tidy key separates every name from its part. The bench cannot measure understanding; it measures the distance and the count of lookups, which is the mechanism the research names.

The nuance: a key is the right tool when the same symbol repeats many times (twenty identical valve icons, one entry in the key), or when parts are too small and dense to label without covering the drawing. On a page with eight distinct parts and room beside each, the key is a tax. The bench's score also gives the key full marks on contrast and overlaps, which is fair: it is tidy. It just is not clear.

LiveThe break button: add decoration2 min▶

Rung 6 adds a mascot block at the top right, slightly larger than the title. Under the entry rule it is now the first thing the eye lands on, so the path runs mascot, title, step 1 and onwards: 1323 px becomes 1506 px. It also sits on the title and the drawing panel, two overlaps. Score 95.9 to 84.2. This is the bench showing it can report a worse number for exactly the move session 1 warned about. If no input could lower the score, the bench would be measuring nothing.

Self-studyWhat the score is not3 min▶

The score is four parts of 25 with weights written on the widget: path = 25 x shortest/actual, minus 5 per crossing; labels = 25 x (1 - distance/300), minus 1 per lookup; contrast = 25 x share of pairs passing; overlaps = 25 minus 6 per pair. Those weights are a teaching model. Nobody has measured that a crossing costs exactly five points of anything. Change the weights and the order of rungs 5 and 6 could change.

What survives any reasonable weighting is the direction of each measurement: direct labels shorten the trip from part to name, a renumbered route stops crossing, a failing pair becomes a passing one, a key on top of a card stops covering it. Argue with the score; trust the measurements. The bench also runs a self-test on load: seven properties, including that black on white is exactly 21:1 and that #777777 on white is 4.48:1, which fails when the sRGB linearisation step is deliberately removed.

Take the six measurements to your own page. You do not need the bench. Draw the numbered route with a pencil and count crossings; measure from each part to its name with a ruler; run every text colour through any WCAG contrast checker; look for anything sitting on anything else. Most pages fail one of the four in under five minutes.

Homework

After the last session

40 min
  1. Beat 95.9 on Wrenmoor, or explain why you cannot. Start from rung 4 and drag cards. Write down the best score you reach and what you moved.
  2. Run Ashbrook's ladder and write one sentence on why renumbering matters more there than on Wrenmoor.
  3. Measure your own one-pager by hand: route crossings, the three longest part-to-name trips, every contrast pair, every overlap.
  4. Change one weight in your head. If a crossing cost 10 points instead of 5, which Ashbrook rungs would move, and would any change order?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On Wrenmoor, "tidy it into a legend" takes the score from 95.9 to 72.7. What did the bench measure that caused the drop?

The tidy key passes contrast and sits clear of the cards, so contrast and overlaps stay at full marks. What it costs is distance and lookups: eight names moved away from their parts. That is the split-attention mechanism, counted.

2 · Ashbrook's step titles are vermilion #AB331C on pale pink #F3CDC2. What does the bench report?

The threshold is a minimum, not a rounding target. 4.45 is a near miss that looks fine by eye, which is exactly why it gets measured. 2.01:1 is Wrenmoor's white on cyan.

3 · Which statement about the bench's score is accurate?

The widget prints the weights and calls them a model. The geometry and the WCAG formula are real arithmetic on a constructed page. Both red rungs prove the score can fall.
