Part 1 · covers spatial contiguity and split attention

Put the name where the part is

5 min live

A key moves the work of joining a word to a picture from the page to the reader. Every lookup is a small trip: hold a letter in memory, cross the page, find it, read the name, cross back, find the part again. Mayer and Moreno's 2003 review names the cure, read at source: place the words next to the part of the picture they describe.

LiveThe spatial contiguity effect, read at source3 min▶

Among their nine ways to reduce cognitive load, Mayer and Moreno (2003) list "aligning words and pictures": put printed text next to the part of the graphic it describes. They call the gain the spatial contiguity effect. The effect size they report is .48, from a single study (Moreno and Mayer 1999, Experiment 1), and they note that "similar effects have been found with text and illustrations in books". One study is a reason to act, not a law: the direction is clear, the size is one measurement.

They define the bad version by example: on-screen text at the bottom of the screen, the graphic at the top. That is exactly a key in the corner. The reader has to scan back and forth, and the scanning uses the same limited attention the explanation needs.

LiveSplit attention and what eyes do2 min▶

Two older lines of work sit behind the effect, both reported here through Mayer and Moreno rather than read at source. Sweller named the cost the split-attention effect: when words and picture are separated, the reader has to hold one while searching the other. Hegarty and Just (1989) recorded eye movements and found that learners read a portion of the text, then look at the matching part of the graphic. Readers already try to join word to part; a key makes each join a journey.

Session 6 measures the journey on Wrenmoor's page. With direct labels, the mean distance from a name to its part falls from 443.8 px to 21.3 px and the number of lookups falls from 8 to 0. Those are bench numbers from a constructed page, measured with a stated model, not readers' eyes.

Self-studyWhen a key is the right call3 min▶

Direct labels are the default, not a rule without exceptions. A key earns its place in two situations:

  • One symbol repeats many times. A map with forty campsites does not print "campsite" forty times; one tent symbol and one line in the key do it once. The lookup happens once, and then the reader knows the symbol everywhere.
  • The parts are too dense to name in place. An exploded view of a gearbox with sixty small parts has no room beside each one. Numbered callouts with a parts list are the honest answer, and the list should sit as close to the drawing as the page allows.

Wrenmoor's cutaway has eight different parts, each appearing once, with room around every one. Neither condition holds, so the key goes. Owen's worry about a cluttered drawing is real, and Part 2 answers it: placement and leader-line habits keep direct labels tidy.

Real world

Transit maps. Station names sit beside the station dot, on the map itself, because a rider looking for a stop is already looking at the line. The line colours, which repeat across hundreds of stations, are the part that gets a key. The same page uses both, each where it belongs.

Part 2 · covers label placement and leader lines

Where a label goes, and how its line behaves

6 min live

Moving names onto the drawing raises the next question: which side of the part? Cartographers have worked on this for a long time, because a map is mostly labels. Their answer is a small set of candidate positions around each point, a preference among them, and a scoring rule for when labels collide.

LiveEight positions, one preference, one score3 min▶

Christensen, Marks and Shieber (1995, read as the MERL technical report TR94-12) set out how cartographic standards treat a point's label. There is a finite set of candidate positions, typically eight around the point, with a ranking of how desirable each one is. A placement is judged on three things together: how much the labels overlap map features and each other, how far each label sits from its preferred position, and how many points are left unlabelled. That scoring idea goes back to Yoeli (1972).

Imhof's 1975 paper "Positioning names on maps" (reported, from secondary summaries) gives the principles behind the preference: names legible, clearly tied to their feature, not covering other content, and for a point, where space allows, the name begins to the right of the symbol. That is the one position preference we rely on. Christensen and colleagues also note that Imhof's analysis is "descriptive, not prescriptive": it records good practice rather than proving an order.

LiveWhy no tool gets it perfect2 min▶

Finding the best placement for every label at once is NP-hard (Marks and Shieber, 1991, cited in the same paper): there is no known shortcut that guarantees the best answer as the number of labels grows. Software therefore approximates, and hand-placing the awkward ones is normal professional practice, not a failure of the tool. Before automation, cartographers placed labels at 20 to 30 per hour (Cook and Jones, 1990, reported in that paper). Eight labels on a one-pager is a few minutes of careful work, and it is worth it.

Self-studySix leader-line habits3 min▶

When a name cannot sit right against its part, a leader line joins them. These habits are working practice across technical illustration and cartography, stated here as craft with no study behind them:

  • Short. The leader is a fallback, not a licence to park labels in a neat column far away; that rebuilds the key one line at a time.
  • Straight, or one bend. A second bend makes the eye follow a route instead of a pointer.
  • Never crossing another line or a word. A crossing is a moment where the reader can follow the wrong line.
  • Ends on the part, with a dot. The dot says "this part", inside its outline; an end in empty space points at nothing.
  • No arrowhead on a label line. On an explainer, arrows already mean flow or sequence (session 2). An arrowhead on a label reads as "water goes here".
  • Horizontal text. Turn the line, never the words; rotated names slow reading.
Real world

The appliance diagram. The best "parts of your coffee machine" diagrams put each name right beside the part with a hairline leader ending in a small dot, all text horizontal, no arrowheads. The weaker ones run every leader out to a tidy column on one side, and the lines fan and cross until the reader has to trace them with a finger, which is the key again in disguise.

Part 3 · covers callouts as signals and contrast

Callouts point, contrast lets them be read

4 min live

A label names a part. A callout says something about one part: a numbered or boxed note tied to it with a short leader. In Mayer and Moreno's terms a callout is a signal, a cue that tells the reader what to select. Signals work by being rare, and every label, line and callout only works if it can be seen against what is behind it.

LiveA callout is a signal, so ration it2 min▶

Mayer and Moreno's signaling method covers cues that tell the learner what to select and how to organise it: stressed key words, arrows, headings. A callout does the same job on a page. It has four parts: a number badge, a short leader to the part, a box, and one sentence. The sentence says something the label cannot, usually why the part matters to the reader.

Signals stop working when everything is signalled. One or two callouts per page is a working limit, a craft judgement rather than a measured threshold. If Nia wants five callouts, the page is trying to be two pages. How much visual weight a callout box should carry against the rest of the page is a hierarchy question, and learn-dashboard-design session 2 owns visual weight; here we own what the callout says and where it points.

LiveThe two contrast numbers, read at source2 min▶

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.3 (level AA) asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for text, and 3:1 for large text, defined as at least 18 point, or 14 point bold. The ratio is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the relative luminance of the lighter colour and L2 of the darker one; relative luminance weights red, green and blue as 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 after an sRGB linearisation step. Text that is pure decoration or part of a logo has no requirement.

Success criterion 1.4.11 (level AA) asks for 3:1 against adjacent colours for "parts of graphics required to understand the content". On an explainer, that covers the leader lines and the part outlines that carry meaning. A faint hairline leader can be elegant and still fail: if the reader needs the line to join the name to the part, the line has to clear 3:1.

Self-studyWrenmoor's and Ashbrook's misses3 min▶

Nia's draft (constructed) set steps 2 and 5 in white on the pale cyan #72C3D4: 2.01:1, well under 4.5:1. The caption under the drawing was the faint grey #D5D8DF on the pale tint #FCEFEA: 1.27:1. The fix is not a new colour scheme; it is the rule on the palette itself: text on cyan is ink, and captions use the muted grey, not the faint one.

Ashbrook Library's draft (also constructed) is the more instructive one. Vermilion #AB331C on the soft pink #F3CDC2 measures 4.45:1 and muted grey #586070 on the same pink measures 4.31:1. Both look perfectly readable on a good screen, and both fail. That is the point: you check contrast with the formula or a checker, not by eye. Choosing a palette is a colour-theory question owned by learn-dataviz; here we only check that the words and lines we placed can be read.

Real world

Museum object labels. Good galleries put a short label right beside each object and save one larger panel for the single idea the room is about. That panel is the callout. When a gallery tries to make every case a feature, visitors read none of them, and pale grey text on a coloured wall is the most common reason a well-written label goes unread.

Build-along

Label Wrenmoor's cutaway on paper

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take Nia's cutaway from session 3 (constructed), or sketch eight boxes in two rows if you missed it. Remove the key, place each name in its first-choice position, fall back only where you must, draw leader lines that cross nothing, add one callout at the chlorine dose, then check every colour. If you have your own one-pager, label it the same way alongside.

List the parts and remove the key. Write the eight names in flow order. Then cross out the key box on the draft with a single line; you are replacing it, not adding to it.

★ Step 1 · the parts1 Reservoir 5 Sand filter 2 Intake screen 6 Chlorine dose 3 Mixing tank 7 Storage tank 4 Settling tank 8 Your tap Key A to H: removed. Each part appears once, room around each: neither "repeated symbol" nor "too dense" applies.

Place every name in its first-choice position. Right of the part, starting just clear of its edge, text horizontal. Do all eight before you fix any collision, so you can see the whole problem at once.

★ Step 2 · first choice: rightfor each part: start the name just right of the part's edge align it with the part's upper half keep it horizontal then look: which names now cover a pipe, a part or another name?

Fall back round the eight positions where a name covers something. On Wrenmoor's cutaway the pipe runs straight through the right side of the chlorine dose and the tap. Try the next positions in turn and take the first one that covers nothing.

★ Step 3 · fall backChlorine dose right covers the pipe -> above Your tap right covers the pipe -> above the other six right is clear -> keep right Score yourself the cartographer's way: overlaps (aim 0) + moved from first choice (2 here) + unlabelled (0)

Draw leader lines only where a name is not touching its part. Short, straight or one bend, ending in a dot on the part, no arrowhead. Check each line against every other line and every word.

★ Step 4 · leader linesChlorine dose name above, gap of a few mm -> one straight vertical line, dot on the part the rest name touches the part: no leader needed Check: does any line cross another line? no does any line pass through a word? no any arrowheads on label lines? no

Add one callout, at the chlorine dose. A number badge just below the part, a short leader to it, and a box with the same number and one sentence. The sentence answers a question a resident actually asks.

★ Step 5 · one calloutbadge 1, below the chlorine dose, short leader with a dot box 1 · Why tap water can smell of chlorine "A little chlorine is left in on purpose, so the water stays safe on its way to you." count callouts on the page: 1 (working limit: one or two)

Check every text and line colour. List each pair of foreground and background you used and check it with a contrast checker or the formula. Text needs 4.5:1, large text 3:1, and any line or outline the reader needs to understand the page needs 3:1.

★ Step 6 · contrastnames ink on paper text, needs 4.5:1 callout text ink and grey on cyan tint text, needs 4.5:1 leader lines ink on paper graphic part, needs 3:1 part outlines ink on paper graphic part, needs 3:1 step boxes ink, never white, on cyan the old 2.01:1 pair, fixed caption muted grey, not faint the old 1.27:1 pair, fixed

Show Owen the before and after side by side. His worry was clutter. Put the two versions next to each other and ask him to name the sand filter on each. On the keyed version he will look for E; on the labelled one he will already be reading the name. That is usually the end of the argument, and session 6 puts numbers on it.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Count the trips on a real page. Find a diagram with a key or legend. Count how many lookups a reader makes to name every part, and decide whether the key is justified: one symbol repeated many times, or parts too dense to name in place.
  2. Relabel your own one-pager. Move every name to its part, first choice right, falling back round the eight positions only where you must. Write down how many names moved from first choice.
  3. Audit your leader lines. Mark every crossing, every arrowhead on a label line, every leader with two bends or more, and every rotated name. Fix each one.
  4. Measure three colour pairs. Pick the three text-on-fill pairs on your page that look weakest and check them against 4.5:1 with a contrast checker. Check one leader line against 3:1. Bring the numbers to session 6.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Owen wants to keep the lettered key on Wrenmoor's cutaway. When would a key genuinely beat direct labels?

A key makes every name a lookup, which is the split attention Mayer and Moreno describe. It pays off when one lookup serves many repeats of a symbol, or when there is no room beside the parts. Wrenmoor has eight different parts, each once, with room around them, so direct labels win.

2 · What does WCAG 2.2 ask for normal text, and for the lines and part outlines a reader needs to understand a graphic?

Success criterion 1.4.3 sets 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text (18 point, or 14 point bold). Success criterion 1.4.11 sets 3:1 for parts of graphics required to understand the content, which covers meaningful leader lines and part outlines.

3 · Why do labelling tools only approximate the best placement, so that designers still hand-place awkward labels?

Christensen, Marks and Shieber work with a finite set of candidates, typically eight per point, scored on overlaps, position preference and points left unlabelled. Choosing the best combination is NP-hard (Marks and Shieber 1991), so tools approximate and hand-placing the hard cases is normal.
