Learn Information Design with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Decoration or explanation

A one-page explainer has a single job: to show a reader how something works, well enough that they could tell someone else. Most first drafts are about their subject rather than explanations of it: a mascot, a stock photo, three fun facts and a logo strip. Tonight we name the job, read the famous argument about decoration at source, three studies that look as if they disagree and mostly measured different things, and leave with one test that every mark on a page has to pass.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Wrenmoor Water is an invented town water utility. After a boil-water notice last winter, its customers kept asking the same question: what actually happens to the water between the reservoir and my tap? Nia, who runs communications, has been asked for a one-page explainer to go on the website, in the welcome pack and on the wall of the visitor centre. Owen, an engineer at the treatment works, knows every step. Nia's first draft is on the table tonight, and over six sessions it becomes an explanation. Wrenmoor, Nia, Owen and every Wrenmoor number are invented for teaching.

Part 1 · covers the one-pager's job

About a subject, or how it works

5 min live

An explainer answers a "how" question: how water gets from a reservoir to a tap, how a vaccine trains the immune system, how a reserved book reaches a branch. The reader should finish able to retell the steps in order. A page that is merely about the subject can be attractive and leave the reader with nothing to retell. The first draft at Wrenmoor is that kind of page.

LiveThe retell test3 min▶

Give the page to someone for thirty seconds, take it away, and ask them to explain how the thing works. If they can only say what the page was about ("it was about water, there was a cartoon drop"), the page decorated. If they can say "it sits in the reservoir, gets screened, the dirt clumps and settles, it is filtered and dosed with chlorine, then piped to you", the page explained.

This is the same test a teacher runs on a lesson, and it is the outcome Mayer's multimedia studies measured: not whether learners liked the material or remembered it, but whether they could use the explanation to solve a new problem (a "transfer" test). That distinction matters in Part 2, because the famous studies on decoration measured different outcomes and are often quoted as if they measured the same one.

Self-studyWhy "about" pages keep getting made3 min▶

A page about a subject is easier to brief and easier to approve. The brand team can check the logo, the comms team can check the tone, and nobody has to agree what the mechanism actually is. An explainer forces that agreement: Nia cannot draw step 3 until Owen tells her whether coagulation happens before or after the water reaches the settling tank. The argument with the expert is the work, and a decorated page is often a way of not having it.

The cure is to write the "how" question at the top of the brief before any design starts. For Wrenmoor: "What happens to the water between the reservoir and your tap?" Every later decision on the page is judged against that question.

Real world

The appliance manual. The safety page at the front of most manuals is about the appliance: warnings, icons, legal lines. The page that shows which way the filter slides out, with an arrow and a numbered sequence, is the one people photograph and keep. Both pages matter; only one of them explains.

Part 2 · covers chartjunk against useful junk

Three studies that measured three different things

6 min live

Edward Tufte named "chartjunk" in 1983: decoration that is non-data ink, which "can turn bores into disasters, but it can never rescue a thin data set" (reported, as quoted by Stephen Few in 2011). Since then two camps have quoted research at each other. Read at source, the studies mostly measured different outcomes, which is why both camps can be right about their own result and wrong about the other's.

LiveMayer: cut what is interesting but extraneous3 min▶

Mayer and Moreno's 2003 review collects nine ways to reduce cognitive load. One is "weeding": remove interesting but extraneous material. Across five experiments at their lab, students who saw a concise narrated animation did better on problem-solving transfer tests than students who saw the same animation with background music or short irrelevant video clips added. The median effect size was .90. They call it the coherence effect: people understand an explanation better when interesting but extraneous material is left out.

Note what this does and does not say. It is about explanations, measured as understanding. The extras were not neutral ornaments on a page; they competed for the same limited attention. It does not test whether a decorated page is remembered longer.

LiveBateman: embellished charts were recalled better3 min▶

Bateman and colleagues (CHI 2010) took fourteen charts by the illustrator Nigel Holmes and made a plain version of each with the same data, titles and axes. Twenty participants described them. Accuracy for the embellished charts was no worse than for the plain ones, participants described the charts' "value message" better for the Holmes versions, and after a gap of two to three weeks they recalled the embellished charts significantly better. Description time did not differ. The authors are "cautious about recommending that all charts be produced in this style".

Stephen Few's 2011 reply, "The Chartjunk Debate", objects that the plain charts were badly designed ("plain ugly"), that twenty university students cannot carry the conclusion, and that Holmes-style charts are typical of only a few news publications. Both papers are worth reading in full; the disagreement is about how far one small study generalises, not about its arithmetic.

Self-studyBorkin: memorable is not the same as understood3 min▶

Borkin and colleagues (InfoVis 2013) collected 2,070 single-panel visualizations and ran a memorability study with 261 Mechanical Turk participants. Colour and a human-recognizable object made a visualization more memorable, and unusual types beat common ones. The authors are explicit that their design treats each visualization as a scene, "does not evaluate the cognitive impact", and that memorability may not translate into understanding, "nor does excessive visual clutter aid comprehension".

Their 2016 follow-up (reported, from the abstract: 393 visualizations, eye movements of 33 participants) found titles and text carry the message, and that pictograms used appropriately do not get in the way and can help recognition. Session 2 uses the title finding.

What the disagreement leaves you with. If the page must be understood, cut what competes with the mechanism (Mayer). If it must also be remembered weeks later, one striking image tied to the message may help (Bateman, Borkin), and it should carry the message rather than sit beside it. Nobody has shown that decoration helps a reader understand a mechanism.

Real world

The design review that went round twice. A familiar meeting: one person says "Tufte says cut it", another says "studies show embellished charts are remembered better", and both are quoting something real. The question that ends it is "what does this page have to do: be understood today, or be recognised next month?" Usually the honest answer is the first, with a little of the second.

Part 3 · covers what earns its place

The four-way mark test

4 min live

Turn the debate into a decision you can make mark by mark. Every mark on an explainer, a line, a box, an icon, a word, a colour, either carries part of the mechanism, tells the eye where to go next, is the one memorable hook tied to the message, or goes.

LiveMechanism first, then signals2 min▶

A mark that shows part of the mechanism stays: the screen in the intake, the tank where floc settles, the arrow that is the water moving. A mark that does not show the mechanism can still earn its place as a signal: Mayer and Moreno's second method for the same overload is to add cues that tell the learner how to select and organise the material, such as headings, an outline, arrows or a map of where you are (session 2 is all signals). Everything else competes with the explanation for the same attention.

Self-studyThe one-hook exception, used honestly2 min▶

The evidence on recall leaves room for one memorable image, provided it carries the message. A cartoon drop in the corner fails that test. The same drop drawn riding through the pipe, stopping at each numbered step, becomes the path itself: a hook and a signal at once. One per page is the working limit; the second mascot is decoration again. This limit is a craft judgement, not a measured threshold, and it is stated as one.

Real world

Safety cards on aircraft. The cards are almost entirely mechanism and signal: numbered panels, arrows showing which way a handle turns, a figure doing each step. There is rarely a mascot. When a page has to be acted on under stress, the industry has already run this test for you.

Build-along

The mark audit on Wrenmoor's draft

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Nia's draft has ten marks (constructed). Number every mark on the page, run each one through the four-way test, and write the verdict beside it. Do the same on any one-pager of your own as we go; if you have none, do Wrenmoor's.

Write the "how" question the page answers. One line, a real question with a sequence for an answer. If you cannot write it, the page has no job yet.

★ Step 1 · the questionWhat happens to the water between the reservoir and your tap? Test: does the answer have steps in an order? yes, six of them

Number every mark. A mark is anything a reader's eye can land on: a box, a picture, a line, a block of words, a colour band. Wrenmoor's draft has ten.

★ Step 2 · the inventory1 title banner "All about water" 6 step text, six lines 2 Drip, a mascot drop 7 arrows along the pipe 3 stock photo of a lake 8 a lettered key A to H 4 three fun-fact tiles 9 step numbers 1 to 6 5 cutaway of the treatment works 10 a border of little drops

Ask the first question of each mark: does it show part of how it works? Only the cutaway and the step text pass. Write "mechanism" beside them.

★ Step 3 · mechanism5 cutaway mechanism: you can point at where the water goes 6 step text mechanism: says what happens at each point everything else: go to question 2

Ask the second question of what is left: does it tell the eye where to go next? Arrows and numbers pass. The title passes only if it is rewritten as the question from step 1, because titles and text are where a visualization's message is carried (Borkin and colleagues, 2016, reported from the abstract).

★ Step 4 · signal7 arrows signal: the direction of flow 9 numbers signal: the order to read in 1 title signal, once rewritten: "From reservoir to your tap: six steps"

Decide the one hook, then cut the rest. Drip can stay only if he carries the message. Everything else goes; the key goes too, because session 5 shows labels beside the parts beat a key on the far side of the page.

★ Step 5 · hook and cut2 Drip one hook: redraw him inside the pipe at step 1 3 photo cut: shows a lake, not the process 4 fun facts cut: true or not, they are not steps 10 border cut: competes for attention, carries nothing 8 key cut: replaced by direct labels (session 5)

Count what is left and check it against the question. Six marks remain, and every one of them helps a reader answer step 1's question. That is the brief for session 3's redraw.

★ Step 6 · the briefKeep: cutaway, step text, arrows, numbers, title as question, Drip in the pipe Cut: photo, fun facts, border, key Retell test after the redraw: can a stranger say the six steps?

Read the cut list to the person who asked for the page. The fun facts and the photo usually came from someone. Give them the reason in their terms: "these are true and they are not the answer to the question the page is for". Offer them a home elsewhere, a website sidebar, a poster, and the page keeps its job.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Run the retell test on a real explainer. Find one one-page explanation at work or online, look for thirty seconds, then write the steps from memory. Note which marks you remembered and which you could not use.
  2. Audit your own page. Number every mark on a one-pager you made and sort them into mechanism, signal, one hook, or cut.
  3. Read one of the three studies at source. Write one line: what outcome did it measure, and what did it not test?
  4. Bring your "how" question. Session 2 builds a reading path from it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A colleague says "studies prove embellished charts are better, so keep the mascot". What is the most accurate reply?

Bateman measured accuracy and later recall on charts, with twenty people; accuracy was no worse and recall better. Mayer measured understanding of explanations, and cutting extraneous material helped. The honest reply keeps both findings in their own lane.

2 · What did Mayer and Moreno's coherence effect actually measure?

Five experiments, narrated animations with or without background music or irrelevant clips, scored on transfer: using the explanation to solve new problems. Median effect size .90. Memory and preference were other studies' outcomes.

3 · On Wrenmoor's draft, the title reads "All about water". Under the four-way test, what happens to it?

A title carries the message (Borkin 2016, reported), so it is a signal, but only if it tells readers what they are about to follow. "From reservoir to your tap: six steps" sets up the path; "All about water" names a subject.
