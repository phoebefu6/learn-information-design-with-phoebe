Part 1 · covers the subject carrying the page

Let the thing itself hold the page

5 min live

On an explainer, the drawing of the mechanism is not an illustration of the text. It is the page. It sits in the middle at full width, it is the largest thing a reader sees, and the words and numbers hang off its parts by short lines. The title sits above it and asks the question the drawing answers.

LiveThe picture replaces the decoration, not the other way round3 min▶

Session 1 cut a stock photo, three fun-fact tiles and a border of drops. That left a hole in the middle of Nia's page, and the temptation is to fill it with something nice. The coherence effect says what to fill it with. Mayer and Moreno (2003) found, across five experiments, that people understood an explanation better when interesting but extraneous material was left out: median effect size .90, measured on transfer tests. The material that helped was the explanation itself.

So the drawing of the mechanism is not an extra competing for space with the explanation. It is the explanation, in the form a reader can point at. Give it the most room on the page. If anything else on the page is bigger than the drawing, ask what that thing explains.

LiveOwen explains, the drawing records2 min▶

Nia does not invent the mechanism; she draws what Owen says. In the constructed walk-through, Owen stands at the reservoir and talks his way along the plant: water is held, then screened, then dosed so the dirt clumps and settles, then filtered through sand, then given a dose of chlorine, then stored and sent down the mains. Nia sketches each part as he names it, in the order he names it, and asks "where does it go next?" at every stop.

The sketch is a record of an explanation, not a picture of a building. That is why the parts sit in a row in the order the water meets them, even though the real plant may be laid out around a yard. Where the water goes is the mechanism; where the buildings stand is not.

Self-studyWhat "carry the page" means at different sizes3 min▶

On a poster for a visitor centre wall, the cutaway can run edge to edge with the step text hanging below it. On a website card at phone width, the same drawing becomes a vertical strip and the step text sits to its right. In a welcome pack folded to A5, the drawing may take the whole inside spread. The rule stays the same at every size: the drawing gets the most area, and every word touches the part it describes. Session 5 is about how words touch parts: leader lines, direct labels and why a key on the far side of the page costs the reader.

If you draw with code rather than a pen, the diagram-as-code route is taught in learn-mermaid; live hand drawing in meetings is learn-sketchnoting. Here we take the side of the finished explainer: one page, one mechanism, drawn to be retold.

Real world

The engine cutaway in a car brochure or textbook. The classic cutaway of an engine fills the page, with small labels on thin lines pointing at the pistons, the valves and the crankshaft. Nobody reads the labels first; they look at the engine and then follow a line to a word when they want a name. That is the order a mechanism page should be read in.

Part 2 · covers three views

Cutaway, exploded view, process

5 min live

There are three standard ways to draw how something works, and each answers a different question. A cutaway answers "what is inside?" An exploded view answers "how does it go together?" A process answers "what happens, in what order?" These are craft categories that illustrators and technical writers use; no single study in the map tests them against each other, so treat them as working vocabulary, not findings.

LiveCutaway: slice the outer wall away2 min▶

A cutaway removes the wall the reader would normally see so that the inside shows, while the outside stays in place for orientation. It answers "what is inside, and where?" For Wrenmoor, the settling tank cut open shows floc sinking to the bottom; the sand filter cut open shows water above a bed of sand, gravel beneath, and an underdrain that collects the filtered water. Without the cut, both are just concrete boxes.

Keep enough of the outside to say what the object is, and cut on the side facing the reader. Label the layers inside, not the wall.

LiveExploded view: pull the parts apart along their axis2 min▶

An exploded view separates the parts along the line they assemble on, in the order they go together, usually with a dashed axis to show they belong in one stack. It answers "how does it go together, and in what order?" Wrenmoor's customers rarely need one for the treatment works, but the leaflet on a dripping tap does: handle, cartridge, washer and body, pulled apart along one axis, tells a reader which part to replace and how to put it back.

The order of the parts on the axis is the assembly order. Get it wrong and the drawing teaches the wrong repair.

Self-studyProcess: stations along a flow, and mixing views3 min▶

A process view lays out stations along a path, numbered in the order something passes through them. It answers "what happens, and in what order?" That is Wrenmoor's question exactly, so the process view is the backbone of Nia's page. But one station, the sand filter, raises a "what is inside?" question that a box labelled "Filter" cannot answer. The standard answer is an inset: a small cutaway of that one station, connected to it by a line.

One backbone view plus at most one or two insets is a working limit, not a measured one. Each inset is a second picture the reader has to connect back to the path, and session 2's route has to carry them there and back. Session 4 adds two more explaining forms, comparison and map, plus the timeline, and asks the same question of each: which reader question does it answer?

Real world

Flat-pack furniture instructions. Assembly sheets are almost entirely exploded views: each panel shows a few parts pulled apart along the line they slide or screw together on, in the order you will use them. They often carry no words at all. When the reader's question is "how does it go together?", the exploded view does nearly all the work.

Part 3 · covers the transformer role (Isotype)

The person between the expert and the artist

5 min live

Isotype, the picture-language developed around Otto Neurath in Vienna, gave a name to the job Nia is doing. Marie Reidemeister, known as Marie Neurath from 1941, was the first and most important "transformer" at the museum in Vienna: she mediated between the scientific advisers who knew the facts and the graphic artists who drew them. Neurath called the role the pivot. Burke and Sandner (2023) suggest it can be seen as a prototype of the modern information designer.

LiveWhat the transformer did2 min▶

At the museum in Vienna, the scientific advisers knew the facts and the graphic artists knew how to draw. Neither, on their own, decided what a chart should say to a visitor. That was the transformer's job: take the advisers' material, decide what the picture must show, and hand the artists a brief they could draw from. Marie Reidemeister did it first and, by Burke and Sandner's account, most importantly. Gerd Arntz, who joined early in 1929, designed the pictograms the charts were built from.

Her later book with Robin Kinross, The Transformer: Principles of Making Isotype Charts (Hyphen Press, 2009), is the place to go for her own account of the work. It is cited here, not summarised: Isotype's detailed rules for charts are outside this session and outside the map.

LiveThe transformer's three questions2 min▶

Whatever Isotype's own procedures were, the role reduces to three questions you can put to any expert. They are a working method, built on the role Burke and Sandner describe, not a quotation from Isotype.

What is the mechanism? Not everything the expert knows: the chain of cause and effect a reader needs to follow. What must the reader take away? The one or two things they should be able to retell. What can be left out? True detail that does not serve the question, which is where the coherence effect comes back: interesting but extraneous material costs understanding, even when it is accurate.

Self-studyNia as Wrenmoor's transformer3 min▶

Owen wants the pumps in, because without them nothing moves. He wants the valves in, because they are where things go wrong. He wants the lab in, because the testing is what makes the water safe to drink. All three are true. None of them answers "what happens to the water between the reservoir and your tap?" in a way a customer can retell. Nia's job is to say so, kindly, and to offer each one a home elsewhere: a visitor-centre panel on testing, a page for engineers on pumping.

The brief she hands the illustrator is short: six stations along one pipe, a cutaway inset of the sand filter, Drip riding the flow, and a list of what is deliberately not on the page. The illustrator does not have to guess what matters, and Owen can check the brief before a line is drawn. If the drawing is generated rather than hand-made, the brief is the same; the craft of generated images and their lettering is taught in learn-ai-illustration.

Real world

The newsroom graphics desk. On many news teams, a graphics editor sits between the reporter who knows the story and the illustrator who draws it. The reporter brings everything; the graphics editor decides what the picture has to show and writes the brief. It is the transformer's job under another name, and it is the part of the work readers never see.

Build-along

Redraw Wrenmoor's page around the mechanism

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You are Nia, and Owen has just walked you through the plant (constructed). On paper, turn his explanation into a page: six sentences, one backbone view with one inset, a list of what stays off, Drip placed in the flow, and a retell test before anything is inked. If you have your own one-pager, run the same six steps on it alongside.

Write Owen's six steps as one sentence each. One sentence per step, subject first, in the order the water meets them. If a step needs two sentences, it is two steps or it holds detail that belongs off the page.

★ Step 1 · six sentences1 Store The reservoir holds the raw water. 2 Screen Intake screens catch leaves and grit. 3 Clump A coagulant makes the dirt clump into floc, which settles in the settling tank. 4 Filter Sand filters trap the rest. 5 Disinfect A dose of chlorine. 6 Deliver A storage tank, then the mains to your tap.

Choose the view by the reader's question. The page's question is about sequence, so the backbone is a process. Then look for the one station where a reader would ask "what is inside?" and give it an inset cutaway.

★ Step 2 · the viewReader's question: what happens, in what order? Backbone view: process strip, six stations Inside question? yes, at step 4: "how does sand clean water?" Inset: cutaway of the sand filter Exploded view? no: nobody is assembling anything

Sketch the pipe with six stations. Draw one pipe across the page, put six numbered stations on it, and hang each one-sentence step off its station. The pipe should be the largest shape on the page.

★ Step 3 · the pipeTitle above: "From reservoir to your tap: six steps" Pipe: left to right, full width, ends at a tap Stations: numbered 1 to 6 on the pipe Step text: under each station, one line, touching it Inset: under station 4, joined by a short line

Decide what to leave out, and write it down. Ask the transformer's third question of everything Owen mentioned. Pumps, valves and lab testing are true and important; they are not needed to answer the page's question. Writing the list protects it when someone asks later why they are missing.

★ Step 4 · left outpumps true; not needed to follow the water valves true; not needed to follow the water lab testing true; belongs on its own panel Test for each: does the reader need it to retell the six steps? no

Place Drip inside the pipe. Session 1 kept one hook, on the condition that it carries the message. Draw Drip once, inside the pipe at station 1, so the mascot becomes the thing being explained: the water travelling. A second Drip anywhere on the page is decoration again.

★ Step 5 · the one hookDrip: one drop, inside the pipe, just after station 1 Not: in a corner, on the title, waving Check: if Drip were removed, would the path still read? yes then Drip is a hook on the path, not the path

Run the retell test on a colleague. Show the sketch for thirty seconds, take it away, and ask them to say what happens to the water. Use someone who is not Owen and has not seen the brief. Where they stall is where the drawing is not yet carrying the mechanism.

★ Step 6 · retell testShow: 30 seconds, then hide the page Ask: "what happens between the reservoir and your tap?" Pass: six steps, in order, in their own words Stall at a step? redraw that station or its sentence first

Show Owen the brief before the drawing. An expert who checks six sentences and a left-out list in five minutes saves a redraw later. It also gives him the chance to argue for the pumps once, on paper, before the illustrator has spent a day on them.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Interview an expert as a transformer. Pick a process at work you do not fully understand. Ask the person who runs it the three questions: what is the mechanism, what must a reader take away, what can be left out. Write their answers as one sentence per step.
  2. Name the view. Find three explainers (a manual, a museum panel, a news graphic) and label each as cutaway, exploded view or process. Write the reader's question each one answers.
  3. Measure the drawing on your own page. On a one-pager you made, roughly shade the area given to the mechanism. Is it the largest thing on the page? If not, write what is bigger and what it explains.
  4. Write a left-out list. For your own page, list three true things you are choosing to leave off, with one line each on why the reader does not need them. Bring it to session 4.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · At the museum in Vienna, what did the transformer do, and who designed the pictograms?

Burke and Sandner (2023) describe Marie Reidemeister as the first and most important transformer, mediating between scientific advisers and graphic artists, a role Neurath called the pivot. Gerd Arntz joined early in 1929 and designed the pictograms.

2 · A reader's question is "how does this go together?" Which view answers it?

An exploded view shows the parts in assembly order along one axis. A cutaway answers "what is inside?" and a process answers "what happens in order?" These are craft categories, not findings from a study.

3 · Owen insists the pumps, valves and lab testing belong on the page because they are true. Why is leaving them out part of drawing the mechanism?

The coherence effect measured understanding on transfer tests: concise explanations beat ones with extraneous material added. Pumps and valves are true, but the reader does not need them to retell the six steps. Isotype has no such six-step rule in the sources here.
