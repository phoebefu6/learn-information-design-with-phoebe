Learn Information Design with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

The reading path

An explainer is read in an order, whether you choose the order or not. If you do not choose it, the reader's eye wanders: it lands on the loudest thing, skims the top, slides down the left edge and leaves. Tonight we decide the order for them: one place to get in, numbers that say what comes next, arrows that say which way things move, and the words for each step sitting right beside the picture of that step.

Part 1 · covers the entry point

Where the eye gets in

4 min live

Every route has a start. On an explainer the start should be the title, written as the question the page answers, because the reader who knows the question knows what they are about to follow. A page with three loud things in three corners has three possible starts, and the reader spends their first look choosing between them instead of reading step 1.

LiveThe title is the entry and the question2 min▶

Borkin and colleagues' 2016 study of 393 labelled visualizations, with eye movements recorded from 33 participants, found that titles and text are where people take a visualization's message from (reported, from the authors' abstract). For an explainer that has a practical consequence: the title is not a label for the page, it is the first instruction. "All about water" tells the reader the topic. "What happens to water before your tap?" tells them there is a sequence coming and that the answer ends at their kitchen.

Write the title as the "how" question from session 1's build-along, then make it the largest and highest piece of text on the page. Everything that could compete with it for the first look either goes or gets quieter.

Self-studyOne dominant entry, not three competing2 min▶

Three loud things on one page are three possible starts. The reader picks one, and if it is the lake photo, the route begins somewhere that is not step 1 and has no arrow out of it. The fix is not to make the title louder than everything else by force; it is to remove the competition. In Wrenmoor's draft, the stock photo is already cut and Drip is already moving inside the pipe, so the title is left alone at the top.

What makes one element win the first glance on a screen, and how to test it by squinting, is taught in learn-dashboard-design session 2: Hierarchy and the first glance ↗. We take the other half: the guided path through an explanation once the eye is in. They answer "where does the first glance land?"; we answer "and where does it go next, and after that?".

Real world

The flat-pack instruction sheet. Most assembly sheets open the same way: one large picture of the finished item, the parts list, then box 1. There is no second hero image fighting the first. The reader knows what they are building and where to start, and the sheet's whole design follows from that.

Part 2 · covers numbering and arrows

Numbers say the order, arrows say the flow

6 min live

Once the reader is in, two signals carry them. Numbers answer "what next?" and arrows answer "which way does the thing move?". They are different jobs: on Wrenmoor's page the numbers give the order of the steps and the arrows show the water travelling along the pipe. Lay the numbers out so the route never crosses itself, because every crossing makes the reader stop and search.

LiveSignaling: what the study found, and how big it was3 min▶

Mayer and Moreno's 2003 review lists signaling as one of nine ways to reduce cognitive load: give the learner cues for how to select and organise the material. The cues in the study were stressed key words in the narration, red and blue arrows on the diagram, an outline with headings, and a small map showing which part of the system was on screen. The evidence they report for it is one study: Mautone and Mayer (2001), Experiment 3, an explanation of how an airplane wing produces lift, with an effect size of .74.

Read that honestly. It is a solid effect in one experiment with one topic, not a law of design. It is still the best evidence there is that numbers, arrows and headings do measurable work for a learner, and it matches what every instruction-writer already does. Use it, and say "one study" when you quote it.

LiveWhy a crossing costs the reader2 min▶

A numbered route works because the next number is always nearby. At a crossing that stops being true: the reader at step 1 sees two lines leaving the same patch of page, and has to read the numbers at the ends of both to find out which line is theirs. On the zigzag above, the reader finishes Store, follows the diagonal down, and on the way meets the 3 to 4 line coming the other way. Every crossing is a small search, and a page with several turns reading into a puzzle.

The snake removes the problem by layout alone: 1-2-3 along the top, drop down at the end, 4-5-6 back along the bottom. Every arrow is short and lands on a neighbour. Session 6 measures crossings and path length on Wrenmoor's draft; in its table, the draft as delivered has 1 crossing and a 1514 px path, and after renumbering the path the route has 0 crossings and a 1323 px path.

Self-studyNumbers and arrows are different signals2 min▶

It is tempting to let arrows do both jobs. Resist it on a process page. The numbers belong to the reader: "this is the third thing to read". The arrows belong to the subject: "the water moves this way". On Wrenmoor's page they agree almost everywhere, which is why the pipe can carry both. On a cycle, a feedback loop or a page where something flows backwards, they disagree, and a reader with only arrows cannot tell where to begin.

Two working rules, both craft rather than measured thresholds: number every step that has to be read in order, and draw an arrow only where something actually moves. An arrow that means "and then" on one part of the page and "flows into" on another is two signals wearing one shape.

Real world

Transit maps. Designers of metro diagrams spend much of their effort on where lines cross, because a crossing is where a passenger has to stop and check which line is theirs. They straighten lines, space stations evenly and keep crossings to the places where a real interchange exists. An explainer's route deserves the same care, with the advantage that you can usually remove every crossing.

Part 3 · covers contiguity and the F-pattern

Put the words where the step is

5 min live

A route only works if the reader can stay on it. The fastest way to throw them off is to put the words for each step somewhere else: a block of text at the bottom, a key in the corner. The evidence says keep the words next to the picture they describe. And the famous F-shaped reading pattern turns out to be what readers do when a page gives them no route at all.

LiveSpatial contiguity and split attention2 min▶

Mayer and Moreno (2003) describe a separated presentation by example: text at the bottom of the screen, the graphic at the top. The learner has to read a line, hold it in mind, find the matching part of the picture, then go back for the next line. Placing the text next to the part of the graphic it describes gave the spatial contiguity effect, an effect size of .48 in a single study (Moreno and Mayer, 1999, Experiment 1), and they note that similar effects have been found with text and illustrations in books.

The cost they are removing has a name: Sweller called it the split-attention effect (reported, via Mayer and Moreno). Hegarty and Just's 1989 eye-movement work (also reported via Mayer and Moreno) shows what readers do with a separated layout: read a portion of text, then look across for the matching part of the graphic, again and again. Every one of those trips is a chance to lose the route. Put "Clump · floc forms and settles" beside the settling tank and the trip is zero.

LiveThe F-pattern, read at source2 min▶

Nielsen's 2006 article for Nielsen Norman Group reported eyetracking of 232 users looking at thousands of web pages. Their gaze often formed an F: a horizontal sweep across the top, a shorter sweep lower down, and a vertical scan down the left side. Nielsen called it "a rough, general shape", not a fixed template.

Pernice's 2017 follow-up is the one to quote. The F-pattern is the default when there are no strong cues: a wall of text with no formatting, a user who wants to be efficient, and who is not interested enough to read every word. NN/g calls it bad for users and for businesses, because whatever sits outside the F is missed. They also documented other patterns readers fall into (layer-cake, spotted, marking, bypassing, commitment), and the fix they recommend is cues: headings, bold key phrases, grouping, bullets.

So the working lesson is the reverse of "put the important thing top left". The F is what happens when a page imposes no route. Impose one: a title as the entry, numbers for the order, words beside their step.

Self-studyWhat about the Z-pattern?1 min▶

You will hear that sparse pages are read in a Z: across the top, diagonally down, across the bottom. It is a designer's rule of thumb, and there is no study behind it among the sources read for this session, so treat it as a habit some designers find useful, not a finding. If you notice that a snake layout looks like a Z on its side, that is because the snake imposes its own route; it does not rely on any default pattern of the eye.

Real world

The recipe card. Old cookbooks often list every ingredient at the top and every instruction in one long paragraph below, so the cook reads a line, looks up for the quantity, looks at the pan, and loses their place. Many newer recipe cards put the quantity inside the step that uses it ("add 2 eggs and whisk"). That is spatial contiguity in a kitchen, and cooks feel the difference without knowing its name.

Build-along

Lay Wrenmoor's reading path

★ 22 min · everyone builds

On a sheet of paper, landscape, lay out Wrenmoor's one-pager (constructed). You are not drawing the cutaway yet, that is session 3; each step's picture is a box with a rough sketch in it. You are deciding where everything sits and in what order it is read, then proving the route with a pencil.

Write the title as the question. Across the top of the sheet, in the largest letters on the page. It is the entry and the promise: the reader will finish knowing the answer.

★ Step 1 · the titleBefore: All about water After: What happens to the water between the reservoir and your tap? Test: is it a question? yes does the answer have steps? yes, six

Pick the single entry and quiet everything else. Look at the sheet as if for the first time. Anything that could pull the first look away from the title gets cut or made smaller. On Wrenmoor's page, Drip moves into the pipe at step 1, so he leads into the route instead of competing with it.

★ Step 2 · one entryEntry: the title, top of the page Competing: stock photo already cut in session 1 Drip now inside the pipe at step 1 logo strip small, bottom corner Check: is there one obvious place to start? yes

Place six cards in a snake. Three across the top, three across the bottom. Write the step names in the order Owen gave them, top row left to right, then bottom row right to left.

★ Step 3 · the snaketop row Store -> Screen -> Clump | bottom row Deliver <- Disinfect <- Filter Rule: the turn happens at the end of a row, never in the middle

Number them, then draw arrows along the pipe. A filled number badge on each card's picture. Then the arrows: short, each one landing on the next card, drawn as the water moving, not as decoration between boxes. Add one more arrow from the title down to step 1.

★ Step 4 · numbers and arrowsNumbers: 1 Store 2 Screen 3 Clump 4 Filter 5 Disinfect 6 Deliver Arrows: title -> 1, 1 -> 2, 2 -> 3, 3 -> 4 (the drop), 4 -> 5, 5 -> 6 Check: does every arrow land on its neighbour? yes

Move each step's words next to its picture. If your draft has the step text in a block at the bottom, cut it up and put each line inside its card, beside the picture. Two short lines per step is plenty; the detail belongs to the drawing in session 3.

★ Step 5 · words beside the step1 Store reservoir holds the raw water 2 Screen intake screens catch leaves and grit 3 Clump coagulant makes dirt clump into floc, which settles 4 Filter sand filters trap the rest 5 Disinfect a dose of chlorine 6 Deliver storage tank, then mains to your tap (simplified teaching version; Owen checks the wording)

Trace the route with a pencil and count crossings. Start at the title and follow the arrows without lifting the pencil. Every time your line crosses one it already drew, make a mark. The target is zero. If you get one, renumber the cards; do not redraw the page.

★ Step 6 · the tracePencil from title -> 1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 4 -> 5 -> 6 Crossings counted: 0 (target 0) Searches for "what next?": none Bring this sheet to session 3.

Hand the sheet to someone who has not seen it. Ask them to point at where they start and then point at each step in turn, without you saying anything. If they hesitate anywhere, that is a place where the route asks them to search. Fix that spot first.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Trace the route on a real explainer. Find a one-page explanation at work or online, follow its numbers or arrows with a pencil, and count the crossings. Note every place you had to search for what comes next.
  2. Rewrite one title as its question. Take a title that names a topic ("Our onboarding process") and rewrite it as the "how" question the page answers.
  3. Find words far from what they describe. One page where the explanation sits at the bottom, in a key or on a separate sheet. Sketch where each piece of text should move to sit beside its part.
  4. Bring your drawing for session 3. Wrenmoor's traced sheet, or your own page laid out the same way. Session 3 fills the picture boxes with the mechanism itself.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What is signaling, and how strong is the evidence for it?

Mayer and Moreno (2003) report signaling from one study, Mautone and Mayer (2001), Experiment 3, on airplane lift, effect size .74. Quote it as one study. Placing text beside its picture is spatial contiguity (.48, single study); .90 is the coherence effect from session 1.

2 · When does the F-pattern appear, according to NN/g's 2017 article, and what do they recommend?

Pernice (2017) describes the F as the default when there are no strong cues, calls it bad for users and businesses, and recommends formatting cues. The F is a symptom of a page with no route, not a layout to design for.

3 · Nia's draft numbers the cards so that the line from step 1 to step 2 crosses the line from step 3 to step 4. Why does that cost the reader?

A numbered route works because the next number is always nearby. A crossing breaks that and turns reading into searching. Renumbering into a snake removes it; session 6 measures crossings and path length on this exact draft.
