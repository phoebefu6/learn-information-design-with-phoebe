Learn Information Design with Phoebe · Session 4 of 6

Five explaining forms

Most explainers come in one of five shapes: a process, an anatomy or cutaway, a comparison, a map or a timeline. Each one is good at answering exactly one kind of question, and each one is bad at the other four. Tonight we name the question behind each form, learn to pick the form from the reader's question rather than from whatever material is lying around, keep one primary form per page, and draw a firm line where explaining ends and charting begins.

Part 1 · covers the five forms and their questions

Each form answers one question

5 min live

A form is not a style; it is a promise about what kind of answer the page holds. A process promises an order. An anatomy promises what is inside. A comparison promises the same yardstick held against two things. A map promises where. A timeline promises when. Five forms, five questions. This is a craft framework, stated as one: no single study defines it, and practitioners carve the family in slightly different ways. It earns its place because it turns "what should this look like?" into a question you can actually answer.

LiveProcess and anatomy: order, and insides2 min▶

A process answers "what happens, in what order?" Its marks are steps and the arrows between them, and its promise is that reading in sequence gives you the story. Wrenmoor's six steps (Store, Screen, Clump, Filter, Disinfect, Deliver) are a process. If the reader would be lost after shuffling the steps, you have a process.

An anatomy or cutaway answers "what is it made of, and what is inside?" Its marks are parts and their names, and its promise is that you can point at each part. A sand filter cut open to show water, sand, gravel and outlet is an anatomy. Nothing moves in a pure anatomy; time is frozen so the parts can be seen. Session 3's cutaway of the works is a hybrid on purpose: an anatomy that a process walks through.

LiveComparison, map, timeline: side by side, where, when2 min▶

A comparison answers "how do these differ?" Its discipline is that both things are held against the same attributes, in the same order, so the eye can travel straight across. Two treatment options described in two separate paragraphs are not a comparison; the same two options in two columns against space, upkeep and what they catch are.

A map answers "where are things, and how are they connected in space?" A map can be geographic (the town, the reservoir, the mains) or schematic, like a metro map that keeps the connections and lets the distances go. A timeline answers "when did things happen, and how long between?" Its marks are events on a line of time. The day of a boil-water notice, issued in the morning and lifted that evening (constructed hours), is a timeline.

Self-studyWhy name forms by questions, not by looks3 min▶

Galleries of explainers usually sort them by appearance: isometric, flat, illustrated, iconographic. That sorting helps when you are choosing a style and fails when you are choosing a structure, because two pages that look alike can answer different questions. Sorting by question makes the form testable: write the reader's question, then ask whether the page's structure is the shape of its answer.

Be honest about the edges. The five-form family is a working framework, stated rather than measured. Some practitioners split "process" into cycles and flows, or fold "map" into a broader "structure" family; a family tree or an org chart sits awkwardly between anatomy and map. When a brief does not fit, do not force it. Name the question first, and the form usually follows.

Real world

The metro map. Most city transit maps drop real distances and real geography and keep only the order of stations and where lines connect. They are maps of connection, built for the question a rider actually asks: "where do I change, and how many stops?" A street-accurate map of the same lines answers a different question, and riders who are changing trains tend to find it harder to use.

Part 2 · covers picking one

Pick the form by the question, not the material

5 min live

Owen sends Nia whatever he has: a plant schematic, a spreadsheet of dosing times, photos of the filters, a map of the mains. The material is tempting because it exists. But material does not choose the form; the reader's question does. Write the question in the reader's words, run it down a short tree, and land on one primary form. Everything else on the page either serves that form as a small inset or leaves.

LiveWalk the tree with a real question2 min▶

A customer writes: "Why was I told to boil my water on Tuesday, and when was it safe again?" Step one, write it down in their words. Step two, the first split: is it about order or time, or about parts or places? It is about time. Step three: does it want the sequence of what happens, or when, and how long between? It names two moments, so it is a timeline.

Now notice what Nia did not do. She did not reach for the plant schematic because Owen had one ready, and she did not open with the map of the mains because maps look impressive. The tree is short on purpose: three levels and five outcomes is about as much as anyone will run in their head during a meeting.

LiveOne primary form, others as insets2 min▶

Real briefs often want two forms. "What happens between reservoir and tap" is a process, but the reader also wonders what a sand filter looks like inside. The fix is not two equal halves. It is one primary form that owns the page and the reading path, plus a small inset that answers the side question right next to the step it belongs to: a cutaway of the filter, tucked beside step 4.

The working rule is one primary form per page, with any other form at inset size. That rule is craft. The nearest evidence comes from Mayer and Moreno (2003): when on-screen text is placed apart from the part of the graphic it describes, learners have to scan back and forth, and placing the words near the matching part gives the spatial contiguity effect, an effect size of .48 in a single study (Moreno and Mayer 1999, Experiment 1). Sweller called the cost of separation the split-attention effect (reported, via Mayer and Moreno). Those studies measured words separated from pictures, not two forms competing on one page; we extend the reasoning, and say so.

Self-studyWhy two primary forms split attention3 min▶

Picture a page with a process along the top and a map of the same pipes filling the bottom, both full size. The reader reads step 3 above, then hunts for where step 3 happens below, then comes back for step 4. That is the back-and-forth scanning Mayer and Moreno describe for separated text and graphics, and the eye-movement pattern they report from Hegarty and Just (1989): read a piece of text, then look for the matching part of the picture (reported, via Mayer and Moreno). Two full-size forms build that search into the page.

The cure, in practice, is either to draw one form inside the other (the process steps ride on the map, so there is only one place to look) or to shrink the second form to an inset parked beside the step it serves. Either way the reader has one route. The coherence effect from session 1 points the same way: material that does not serve the question competes with it, and cutting it helped understanding, median effect size .90 over five experiments (Mayer and Moreno, 2003).

Real world

Flat-pack furniture instructions. The primary form is a process: numbered panels, one action each. When a step needs you to know which screw, the parts appear as a small anatomy inset inside that panel, drawn at true size, not as a separate parts poster you have to keep turning back to. One route, with insets riding along it.

Part 3 · covers the seam with charts

Never a chart here

5 min live

One kind of question never lands on any of the five forms: "how much?", or "how has a quantity changed?" That question wants a chart, and chart form, marks, colour and honesty are the craft of learn-dataviz. We take the other side of that seam: explaining how something works. A comparison of two water-treatment options by their attributes is ours; the trend in litres per day is a chart. And a timeline here is a line of events, never a line chart of a measure.

LiveThe seam, stated as a test2 min▶

Ask one question of any brief: is the answer a quantity, or how a quantity changed? If yes, it is a chart, and the decisions that matter (which chart form, which marks, which scale, how to keep it honest) are the craft of learn-dataviz. If no, it is one of the five explaining forms, and it stays here.

The test is about the answer, not about whether numbers appear. A comparison of two treatment options can hold a few values in its cells and still be a comparison, because the reader's question is "how do these differ, attribute by attribute?" The moment the question becomes "how many litres per day, month by month?", the answer is the shape of a quantity, and that shape is a chart.

LiveA timeline is events, not a measure2 min▶

The most common mix-up at this seam is the timeline. A timeline in an explainer is a line of time with events on it: the notice issued, the tankers out, the tests clear, the notice lifted. Its question is "when, and how long between?" A line that rises and falls with the chlorine level through the day looks similar, because it also has time along the bottom, but its question is "how much, and how did it change?" That is a line chart of a measure, and it belongs across the seam.

A useful check: if you could delete the vertical axis and nothing would be lost, you have a timeline. If deleting it destroys the answer, you have a chart.

Self-studyWhen a brief wants both sides3 min▶

Finance asks Nia for "how our treatment works, and how much we treated last year". That is two questions on one sheet. Split it. The explainer answers the first; a chart, made with chart craft, answers the second, and sits on its own panel or its own page. Folding a monthly volume line into the corner of the process diagram gives a chart without a chart's care and a process with a second primary form.

Neighbouring seams follow the same logic. How visual weight pulls the eye on a screen is learn-dashboard-design session 2; how a story runs across several slides is learn-visual-storytelling. We stay on the single page that explains how something works.

Real world

The water bill. Many utility bills carry two graphics side by side: a small bar chart of your use over recent periods, and a drawing of the meter showing which dial to read. The first answers "how much?" and is a chart. The second answers "what am I looking at, and in what order do I read it?" and is an anatomy with a short process. Good bills keep them apart and let each do its own job.

Build-along

Pick the form for three briefs

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Four briefs are on Nia's desk (all constructed): three from Wrenmoor customers and colleagues, one from Ashbrook Library, a neighbouring service Nia is helping out. One of them is a trap. For each, write the question, walk the tree, name one primary form and any inset, and sketch a thumbnail no bigger than a playing card. Thumbnails, not drawings: boxes, arrows and words only.

Write each brief as the reader's question. Not the requester's title, the reader's actual question, in their words. Four lines, one per brief.

★ Step 1 · four questions1 Wrenmoor "What happens between the reservoir and my tap?" 2 Wrenmoor "Why does my water taste of chlorine some mornings?" 3 Ashbrook "How does a book I reserved reach my branch?" 4 Wrenmoor "How many litres did Wrenmoor treat each month?"

Run the seam test first. Before the tree, ask of every brief: is the answer a quantity, or how a quantity changed? Brief 4 is. It leaves the page now, with a short note to the requester, so it cannot drag a chart into an explainer later.

★ Step 2 · the trapBrief 4: answer is a quantity by month -> a chart Send to: learn-dataviz craft (form, marks, honesty) Note to finance: "This is a chart, not an explainer; it gets its own panel." Briefs 1 to 3: no quantity in the answer -> walk the tree

Brief 1: walk the tree and name the inset. Order or time? Order. What happens next? Process. Readers also ask what a sand filter is, so the anatomy rides along as an inset beside step 4, not as a second half.

★ Step 3 · reservoir to tapPrimary form: process, six steps 1 Store 2 Screen 3 Clump 4 Filter 5 Disinfect 6 Deliver Inset: cutaway of the sand filter, parked beside step 4 Thumbnail: six numbered circles along a pipe, small box under step 4

Brief 2: notice the question is "why", and find the part. The taste comes from the chlorine dose, so the reader first needs to see where the dose goes in: an anatomy of the dosing point. A short timeline underneath shows the dose travelling from the works through the mains to the tap. No numbers on it: it is about when the water passes each point, not how much chlorine is in it.

★ Step 4 · the chlorine tastePrimary form: anatomy of the dosing point (Chlorine dose -> Storage tank -> mains) Inset: short timeline, three events: dose added, water in the mains, water at your tap Not drawn: chlorine level over the morning -> that is a measure, so a chart Thumbnail: pipe with the dose box above it, three-tick line below

Brief 3: draw one form inside the other. Ashbrook's question is a process (reserve, pulled from the shelf, onto the van, arrives at your branch), but each step happens somewhere. Instead of a process strip plus a separate map, put the numbered steps on the route map. One place to look, one route to follow.

★ Step 5 · Ashbrook (constructed)Primary form: process, four steps, drawn on the route map 1 Reserve 2 Pulled from the shelf 3 On the van 4 At your branch The map is the ground; the numbers are the path Thumbnail: faint map box, route line, four badges along it

Check every thumbnail against the one-primary rule. Cover the inset with your thumb. If the page still answers its question, the primary form is doing its job. If it does not, you drew two primaries; shrink one or merge them.

★ Step 6 · the thumb testBrief 1: cover the cutaway -> six steps still answer "what happens" pass Brief 2: cover the timeline -> dosing point still answers "why" pass Brief 3: one form inside the other, nothing to cover pass Session 5 labels all three thumbnails.

The trap brief is a kindness, not a refusal. When a request lands on the wrong side of the seam, give it a better home rather than a "no": "This is a chart, and it deserves chart craft; here is who builds it." Requesters rarely care which form they get; they care that their question is answered well.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Owen wants visitors to understand what is inside the sand filter: the layers and where the water leaves. Which form answers "what is inside"?

"What is it made of, what is inside?" is the anatomy question. A process could ride on it later as an inset, but the reader's question here is about parts, so the cutaway is primary.

2 · A manager asks Nia for "an infographic showing how much daily water use has changed since the new meters went in". What should she do?

"How much has X changed" is a quantity question. It crosses the seam to charts, where learn-dataviz owns the decisions. Tucking a trend line into a process gives a chart without chart craft and a page with two primary forms.

3 · Why keep one primary form per page, with any second form at inset size?

The evidence is about separated words and pictures (spatial contiguity, .48 in a single study; split attention, reported via Mayer and Moreno). Extending it to two competing forms is a reasoned craft rule, and it is stated as one. No study measured "two forms halve comprehension".
